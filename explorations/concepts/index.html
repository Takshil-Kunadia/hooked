Hooked · story concepts · pick a world

One moment, four worlds.

Every frame below shows the same two ideas. First the init action: WordPress announces "init is happening" and every listener runs in priority order. Then the the_title filter: a value passes through each callback and comes out changed. Whichever world we pick gets used for every chapter, so the story stays consistent.

Metro line My pick new · beginner-first

The request is a train, and the hooks are stations along its line. At an action station the train announces itself, and everyone waiting on the platform does their job in ticket order (priority). At a filter workshop each station takes the cargo, changes it and puts it back. The database is the depot, and the template hierarchy is a junction where signals pick the first platform that exists.

Why it worksEveryone has used a metro map, so there's nothing new to learn. "Hooks are stops where your code can hop on" is easy to hold onto. It keeps the 3D motion you liked from the marble. Other entry points (admin, REST, AJAX, cron) become other lines merging into the same trunk.
Watch out forAnalogies drift. Every sign uses the real WordPress name, and the developer layer shows the actual code, so the metaphor never replaces the truth.

Dev workspace you asked how it looks

Porcelain 3D versions of things developers use every day. The request is an HTTP request card riding a pipeline. An action is a notification bell over an editor window: callbacks are lines, the line-number gutter is the priority, and a debugger cursor ▶ steps through them. A filter is a pipe chain ($title | wptexturize | …).

Why it worksIt's native to developers and literally accurate, since WordPress really stores callbacks by priority number. All the text is easy to read, and it builds skills that carry over to debugging real code.
Watch out forIt feels more like a UI than a world, so there's less of the magical 3D motion. To someone who has never coded it can look "techy" and intimidating.

Marble run + dev props evolves round 2

Keep the marble and the track you liked, and swap every prop for a dev object. The bell becomes a notification-bell icon with a subscriber count. Callbacks are a "subscribed" list with priority gutters. The tubes become labelled pipeline segments.

Why it worksIt's the least rework, and it keeps the playful motion. The labels and lists carry the accuracy.
Watch out forThe metaphor is mixed: a toy marble next to UI props. A first-timer will still ask "why is a page request a marble?"

Package line new · beginner-first

Your web page is a package being assembled on a conveyor. Items are pulled from the warehouse (the database). A speaker announces moments ("init!"), and workers act on them in number order. Filters are stamping stations that change the package label. At the end, the package ships to your browser.

Why it worksYou literally watch the page get built, which makes filters and rendering very clear. Blocks become boxes inside boxes. It's very tangible for first-timers.
Watch out forA factory can look busy and industrial, which is harder to keep Apple-sleek. Actions (announcements) and filters (stations) also use two different props.

The whole story for a first-timer (metro version)

STOP 0 · NEW CHAPTER
Before WordPress
You type a URL. Your browser buys a "ticket" (the request) and sends it to a server, the computer that hosts the site.
STOP 1
The front door
index.php is the station every visit arrives at first.
STOP 2
The control room
wp-config.php knows where the database is and holds the site's secret keys.
STOP 3
Waking up
wp-settings.php is the big interchange. The plugins line and the theme line join here.
STOP 4
The first big announcement
init: everyone who subscribed with add_action gets their turn, in ticket order.
STOP 5
The depot
WP_Query fetches your posts from the database.
STOP 6
The junction
The template hierarchy: signals send the train to the first platform that exists, like single.php.
STOP 7
The workshops
Filters like the_title change what's on board before it's shown.
STOP 8
Back home
200 OK: the finished HTML arrives in your browser, and WordPress cleans up (shutdown).
